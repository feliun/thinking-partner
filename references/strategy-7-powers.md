# 7 Powers — Strategy Reference

> Source: *7 Powers: The Foundations of Business Strategy* by Hamilton Helmer

## Core Framework

**Strategy** = the study of the fundamental determinants of potential business value.

**Power** = the set of conditions creating the potential for persistent differential returns.

**Potential Value = [Market Scale] × [Power]**

Power has two components:
- **Benefit** — must materially augment cash flow (increased prices, reduced costs, and/or lessened investment needs)
- **Barrier** — must prevent competitors (existing and potential) from engaging in value-destroying arbitrage

The **3 S's of Power**: Power is created when a business attribute is simultaneously:
1. **Superior** — improves free cash flow
2. **Significant** — the cash flow improvement must be material
3. **Sustainable** — the improvement must be largely immune to competitive arbitrage

**Surplus Leader Margin (SLM)** = the profit margin the business with Power can achieve if pricing is such that its competitor's profits are zero. Formula: `SLM = [C/(Leader Sales)] × [(Leader Sales)/(Follower Sales) – 1]`

## The 7 Powers

### 1. Scale Economies

> Declining unit costs with increased business size.

- **Benefit:** Reduced costs. The leader's scale translates into lower per-unit costs.
- **Barrier:** Prohibitive cost of share gains. Smaller competitors face a lose-lose: match pricing and bleed, or differentiate and lose share.
- **Sources:** Fixed cost spreading, volume/area relationships, distribution network density, learning economies, purchasing economies.
- **Key insight:** Power is a *potential* for value — it must be coupled with operational excellence to be realized.
- **SLM formula:** `Surplus Leader Margin = [Scale Economy Intensity] × [Scale Advantage]`

### 2. Network Economies

> The value of the service to each customer is enhanced as new customers join the network.

- **Benefit:** A leader can charge higher prices due to higher value from more users (e.g., LinkedIn's HR Solutions Suite derives value from LinkedIn's user base).
- **Barrier:** The unattractive cost/benefit of gaining share — extremely high.
- **Characteristics:** Winner-take-all dynamics, tipping points, boundedness (bounded by the character of the network — Facebook and LinkedIn coexist because they serve different networks).
- **Key insight:** Decisive early product matters. Due to tipping point dynamics, early relative scaling is critical. The network effect δ must be large enough relative to the installed base and cost structure.

### 3. Counter-Positioning

> A newcomer adopts a new, superior business model which the incumbent does not mimic due to anticipated damage to their existing business.

- **Benefit:** The new model is superior — lower costs and/or ability to charge higher prices.
- **Barrier:** Collateral damage. The incumbent rationally chooses not to adopt because it would cannibalize their existing business. This is thoughtful calculation, not ignorance.
- **Key conditions:** (1) The challenger's approach is novel and unproven, shrouded in uncertainty. (2) The incumbent has deeply embedded routines and a successful model — their heritage creates cognitive bias.
- **Important distinction:** Power is relative to *each* competitor. Counter-Positioning only applies relative to the incumbent — says nothing about power relative to other challengers.
- **vs. Disruptive Technologies:** Not the same. Kodak didn't fail because of Counter-Positioning — digital photography as a standalone business offered no Power for Kodak. Counter-Positioning requires the new model to actually be *superior*.
- **Key insight:** A competent Counter-Positioned challenger must take advantage of the incumbent's strengths — it is this strength that creates the Barrier (collateral damage).

### 4. Switching Costs

> The value loss expected by a customer that would be incurred from switching to an alternate supplier for additional purchases.

- **Benefit:** Can charge higher prices to existing customers for equivalent products/services.
- **Barrier:** Competitors must compensate customers for Switching Costs to win them over.
- **Three types:**
  - **Financial** — direct monetary costs of switching
  - **Procedural** — loss of familiarity, risk and uncertainty of the new product
  - **Relational** — breaking of emotional bonds built through use and interactions
- **Non-exclusive Power:** Available to all players — intensity derives from "Industry Economics."
- **Key insight:** Switching Costs offer no Benefit with *potential* customers and no Benefit without follow-on products. Major value comes from capturing customers *before* pricing arbitrage kicks in. Develop more add-on products and use acquisitions to accelerate product line extensions.
- **Spillover:** If product preference from existing users spills over to potential customers → Branding effects emerge.

### 5. Branding

> The durable attribution of higher value to an objectively identical offering that arises from historical information about the seller.

- **Benefit:** Able to charge higher prices due to (1) affective valence — good feelings about the offering, and (2) uncertainty reduction — "peace of mind" that the product will be as expected.
- **Barrier:** Hysteresis — a strong brand can only be created over a lengthy period of reinforcing actions, which itself serves as the barrier.
- **Characteristics of strong brands:** Heritage (long positive history), Elegance (consistent aesthetic), Exclusivity (only attainable by those willing to pay for the best), Flawless (repeated perfect products).
- **Challenges:** Brand dilution, counterfeiting, changing consumer preferences, geographic boundaries.
- **Narrowness:** Branding as a Power is much more restricted than "brand recognition" in marketing. High recognition ≠ Branding Power.
- **Requirements for Branding Power:** (1) Type of good — only certain types have Branding potential, (2) Magnitude — must justify a significant price premium, (3) Duration — long enough time to achieve such magnitude.

### 6. Cornered Resource

> Preferential access at attractive terms to a coveted asset that can independently enhance value.

- **Benefit:** Superior deliverables and/or lower costs from the unique resource (e.g., Pixar's cohesive team of animation veterans).
- **Barrier:** Unlike other Powers — the Barrier is *personal choice* or unique circumstance. The resource holder chooses to stay.
- **Five Tests:**
  1. **Idiosyncratic** — if a firm repeatedly acquires coveted assets cheaply, the real question is "why are they able to do this?" (that's a different Power)
  2. **Non-arbitraged** — if you pay full price for the resource, it fails the differential return test
  3. **Transferable** — if the resource only creates value at *this specific* company (due to complements), isolating it as the source of Power overlooks the real driver
  4. Independently value-enhancing
  5. Appropriable at attractive terms

### 7. Process Power

> Embedded company organization and activity sets which enable lower costs and/or superior product, and which can be matched only by an extended commitment.

- **Benefit:** Improved product attributes and/or lower costs from process improvements embedded in the organization.
- **Barrier:** Hysteresis — these advances are difficult to replicate. They can only be achieved over a long time period of sustained evolutionary advance. Two factors create this: **Complexity** and **Opacity**.
- **Key insight:** The rarity of Process Power results from the infrequency of the Barrier — an unyielding, long-time constant for the improvements in question. Process Power is bottom-up emergence, not top-down design.

## Strategy Dynamics (Part II)

Strategy has two dimensions:
- **Statics** ("Being There") — what makes a position durably valuable?
- **Dynamics** ("Getting There") — what developments yield attractive positions?

### The Path to Power

- **"Me too" won't do** — compelling value requires mobilizing capabilities to fulfill a significant customer need currently unmet by competitive offerings.
- **Compelling value** drives customer adoption; Power ensures it's durable.
- Success requires that a company **stay in the game**, appropriately morphing to suit the requirements of the situation.

### The Power Dynamics Toolkit (Appendix 9.1)

**The Value Axiom:** Strategy has one and only one objective — maximizing potential fundamental business value.

## Quick Diagnostic Questions

When analyzing a business for Power, ask:

| Power | Key Question |
|-------|-------------|
| Scale Economies | Do we have meaningfully lower unit costs at scale than competitors? |
| Network Economies | Does each new user make the product more valuable for all existing users? |
| Counter-Positioning | Are we using a superior model that incumbents won't copy because of collateral damage? |
| Switching Costs | Would our customers face real costs (financial, procedural, relational) to switch? |
| Branding | Can we charge a premium for an objectively similar product based on brand perception? |
| Cornered Resource | Do we have preferential access to a valuable resource at non-arbitraged terms? |
| Process Power | Do we have deeply embedded processes that competitors can't quickly replicate? |

## Applying the Framework

When using this framework for strategic analysis, always:

1. **Identify the market** — what is the "significant market" where we compete?
2. **Test each Power** against the dual requirements: Benefit (cash flow) + Barrier (competitor exclusion)
3. **Be honest** — operational excellence is NOT Power. Power is what remains after competitors achieve operational parity.
4. **Think dynamically** — which Powers can we *build toward*, not just which we have today?
5. **Consider relativity** — Power is always relative to specific competitors, not absolute.
